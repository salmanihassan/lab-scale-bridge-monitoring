# lab-scale-bridge-monitoring
Transportation infrastructure—including bridges, rail systems, tunnels, and roadways—forms the backbone of economic activity and public safety in the United States. Traditionally, the safety and reliability of this infrastructure have depended on periodic manual inspections and conservative design margins. In recent years, however, transportation agencies have increasingly adopted continuous monitoring systems based on distributed sensors, embedded controllers, and networked data pipelines. These systems are used to assess structural health, detect early signs of deterioration, inform maintenance planning, and in some cases guide real-time operational decisions such as load restrictions or service interruptions.
While this transition toward data-driven infrastructure management has clear benefits, it also introduces new and underappreciated risks. Many sensing and monitoring systems implicitly assume that the data they receive is accurate and trustworthy. As these systems become connected to networks and cloud-based analytics platforms, they become vulnerable to cyber threats such as sensor spoofing, data replay, timing manipulation, and communication disruption. Unlike traditional failures, these cyber-induced disruptions may not cause obvious alarms or system shutdowns. Instead, they can create false-safe conditions, in which monitoring systems continue to report normal or safe operation while the physical infrastructure is actually experiencing unsafe loading, degradation, or damage.
This project is motivated by the recognition that cybersecurity is now inseparable from transportation safety. A compromised sensing system can lead to delayed maintenance, incorrect safety assessments, or unsafe continued operation—outcomes that directly increase the risk of crashes, service failures, or structural collapse. Addressing these risks requires an approach that goes beyond traditional IT security measures and instead integrates physical system awareness, secure embedded systems, and intelligent data analysis.
To meet this need, the project develops an AI-driven digital twin framework that continuously verifies the trustworthiness of sensor data used in transportation safety monitoring. In this context, a digital twin is not a high-fidelity structural simulation, but a lightweight behavioral reference model that captures how sensors are expected to behave under normal and acceptable physical conditions. By comparing real-time sensor measurements against this expected behavior, the system can identify deviations that are inconsistent with physical reality and therefore indicative of cyber-induced manipulation or abnormal system behavior.
The digital twin serves as the behavioral ground truth against which AI-based anomaly detection operates. Artificial intelligence techniques—specifically lightweight neural and unsupervised learning models—are used to detect subtle patterns associated with spoofing, replay, and timing-based attacks that may evade simple threshold-based checks. Importantly, these AI models are not deployed in isolation; they are anchored to physical constraints and expectations provided by the digital twin, improving robustness, interpretability, and safety relevance.
The project adopts a cyber–physical survivability perspective, emphasizing not only attack detection but also the ability of the monitoring system to continue providing useful and trustworthy information under partial compromise. Rather than assuming perfect operation, the system is designed to degrade gracefully, maintaining situational awareness even when some sensors, communication channels, or computational resources are impaired.
Execution of the project is intentionally interdisciplinary. Mechanical Engineering students focus on sensing, calibration, physical modeling, and validation of what constitutes physically plausible behavior. Computer Engineering students focus on secure embedded firmware, communication integrity, cyber-attack modeling, artificial intelligence, and system-level resilience. Through close collaboration, students learn how physical laws, cybersecurity mechanisms, and data-driven intelligence combine to protect transportation safety.
Overall, this project provides a realistic, hands-on research experience that reflects the challenges faced by modern transportation agencies. It advances practical methods for cyber-resilient infrastructure monitoring while preparing students to work at the intersection of cybersecurity, embedded systems, artificial intelligence, and physical system safety.
